The problem
A museum label gives a name and a date. The object is right there, but its story stays behind the glass.
What I designed
Relique, a museum website where artifacts tell their own life in the first person, with images, text and voice, and where symbols do the explaining a label usually does.
Where it landed
A full website prototype, from a map of museums to stories that lead into each other, and a narrated walkthrough.

Relique reimagines the museum visit as storytelling. Instead of static facts, each artifact tells its own story, so history feels emotional and personal.

A brief in signs, not words.

Relique was made for a course in semiotics and semantics. Semiotics is how signs, shapes and symbols carry meaning; semantics is what the words themselves say. The brief asked for a design where meaning comes from the first, with the second in support.

Aim
Make history immersive by letting artifacts tell their own stories, with emotion and context, so a static display becomes a living one.
Goal
Help people explore museums and rare artifacts around the world through stories that are accurate as well as moving.

It starts with what a visitor sees first.

The home page opens onto the world.

A world map with a single way in, Explore, then featured artifacts, upcoming museum events, and the same home on a phone. Real artifacts sit around the page, cut out, as if on a table.

From the home page, a story is four steps away.

Four steps from the map to a story.

1. Explore opens a map of museums

One tap on Explore turns the home page into a map. Each museum card shows its picture, how many artifacts it holds, and its rating.

2. Every museum has its own page

It splits two ways: the museum’s background, or straight into its collections. Visitors read and write reviews here too.

Why: some visitors want the context first and others want the objects; neither is made to wait.

3. Collections open like drawers

Discover Collections lists Ancient Civilisations, Arms and Armour, Jewellery and more. Each opens to show its pieces.

4. Hover an artifact, and it offers its story

The card turns and reveals My Story, which starts the artifact’s own telling, in pictures, text and voice.

Why: the object becomes a character to meet, not an entry to read.

My Story is where the artifact starts talking.

The artifact tells its own life, in the first person.

A story opens on a title card with the museum, the date and the place, then moves scene by scene, narrated in text and voice. This is the personal armour of Emperor Akbar, in seven scenes.

My Story: Personal Armour of Emperor Akbar

Around the stories, the site explains what Relique is.

The about page says why it exists.

How frame-by-frame storytelling works, how the site was planned, made and launched, and its mission: making history accessible to everyone, whatever their background.

Every one of these pages leans on the same few signs.

Symbols carry what a label would spell out.

Three symbols are used the same way everywhere on the site.

  • A story begins here
  • Open and close a collection
  • Move through a life

Seven places the signs do the work

Where the meaning comes from a sign, from the words, or from both.

  1. The museum card both

    The photo, the info and archive icons and the star rating make a card that reads as something to open before any text is read. The names are the semantics.

  2. Start Story semiotics

    A triangle means play: a story starts here.

  3. An artifact card both

    The object signals that it matters, its name points to a powerful ruler, and its hover state invites a closer look. The description that appears is semantics.

  4. An object inside a story both

    A lit border asks to be hovered. The description that opens is semantics.

  5. The collections semiotics

    A plus means open and a minus means close, so the list reads as an accordion before anyone touches it.

  6. The story viewer both

    The story in the artifact’s own voice, in text and speech, is semantics. The arrows mean back and forward.

  7. The title card semiotics

    Icons stand for the museum, the artifact’s age and its place.

Walk through the website.

From the home page to the armour’s story, then the museum map and a second story, with the artifacts’ narration.

What I learnt.

Signs shape how history is read
Shapes, symbols and story decide how people take in a historical object, emotionally as much as intellectually.
First person makes it personal
Told from the artifact’s point of view, history felt personal and immersive.
Generated images need a firm hand
Every image had to stay consistent in style and accurate in detail, or the stories stopped feeling real.
Hold three things at once
Historical accuracy, an engaging story and an easy interface pull against each other; the design has to keep all three.
